Snyder is a historian of the Holocaust, Nazi Germany, and the Soviet Union. In this 126-page book from early 2017, he argues that Americans are no wiser than the Europeans who watched democracy give way to fascism, Nazism, and communism, and that our one advantage is the chance to learn from what happened to them. His version of the old saying is that history does not repeat, but it does instruct. From that he builds twenty short chapters, each a single instruction for ordinary citizens. The lessons, in the book’s order:

1. Do not obey in advance
2. Defend institutions
3. Beware the one-party state
4. Take responsibility for the face of the world
5. Remember professional ethics
6. Be wary of paramilitaries
7. Be reflective if you must be armed
8. Stand out
9. Be kind to our language
10. Believe in truth
11. Investigate
12. Make eye contact and small talk
13. Practice corporeal politics
14. Establish a private life
15. Contribute to good causes
16. Learn from peers in other countries
17. Listen for dangerous words
18. Be calm when the unthinkable arrives
19. Be a patriot
20. Be as courageous as you can

Each chapter pairs its instruction with a historical example, and the first sets the tone. Snyder calls it anticipatory obedience: citizens adapt to a new regime before anyone has ordered them to, and in doing so show the people in power what they can get away with. His example is Austria in 1938, where people who had never been Nazis started signaling loyalty on their own. The early chapters focus on what individuals and institutions do, and later ones turn to language, truth, personal habits, and the pull of nationalism. Snyder writes in short, direct sentences, so the book reads fast. I picked it up nine years after publication, and it holds up.

I’m a librarian, so the chapters on defending institutions and remembering professional ethics hit closest to home. Snyder argues that it is hard to subvert a rule-of-law state without lawyers or to stage show trials without judges. He says the Nazi regime would have struggled to carry out its atrocities if lawyers, doctors, businesspeople, and bureaucrats had refused to take part, and that professional ethics matter most when someone insists the situation is exceptional. My profession’s code of ethics commits us to intellectual freedom, to resisting censorship, and to protecting patron privacy. Those commitments mean something only when the people working inside the institution keep honoring them under pressure. A library has no way to defend its principles on its own, and that is the point of Snyder’s second lesson.

My one complaint is length. Several chapters end just as Snyder starts getting into the history behind the lesson, and I wanted more of that explanation. Since this is a pocket guide and a deep history was never the goal, I don't fault it for that. It’s a quick read that sticks with you, and I’d recommend it to anyone.

Snyder mentions Hitler 24 times and Putin 12, but I couldn't find Trump's name anywhere in the book. The president who prompted it appears only as "the president," about three dozen times, and most of those references are clearly him. In a book that includes a lesson on listening for dangerous words, the choice to withhold that one stood out to me. Snyder wrote it without naming the man at the center of it, and in 2025 he left the United States for Canada after more than two decades at Yale. That combination stuck with me.
